IELTS Academic exam — quick reference

Format details are occasionally revised — cross-check against the official ielts.org test format page closer to your test date.

IELTS Academic exam sections, timing, and content
SectionTimeContent
Listening 30 min
(+ transfer time)		 40 questions, 4 recordings of increasing difficulty. Shared with General Training. Paper-based tests give 10 minutes to transfer answers to the answer sheet afterward; computer-delivered tests give 2 minutes to review answers typed directly during the recording.
Reading 60 min 40 questions, 3 long academic passages (journal/textbook/magazine style). Question types include multiple choice, matching headings/information, sentence completion, and fill-in-the-blank (summary/note/table completion).
Writing 60 min Task 1 (20 min, ~150 words): describe a chart/graph/table/diagram. Task 2 (40 min, ~250 words): argumentative/discursive essay. Task 2 counts twice as much as Task 1 toward the Writing band.
Speaking 11–14 min 3 parts: introduction/interview, a 2-minute long-turn on a cue card, and a two-way discussion on related themes. Shared with General Training.

Total time: ≈2 hours 45 minutes. Bands are scored 1–9 in 0.5 increments per section, then averaged (rounded) for the Overall Band Score.

Many centres now also offer the One Skill Retake (OSR) — resit just the one section that let you down instead of the whole test, within 60 days, and get a new Test Report Form you can choose to submit alongside (or instead of) the original. Worth knowing before you rely on it: it currently requires your original test to have been computer-delivered (a paper-based original isn't eligible, though centres are gradually extending OSR to the Writing-on-Paper format), the retake has to be booked at an OSR-enabled centre in the same country as the original, and as of early 2026 it isn't offered at all for tests taken in the US — so confirm availability with your test centre rather than assuming it applies to you.

Tips for a Band 8–9 score

Vocabulary alone won't get you there — Band 8–9 is mostly about disciplined technique under time pressure. These are the highest-leverage, most consistently-cited habits per section.

Listening

  • Use the 30–45 seconds before each section starts to read the questions and predict the type of answer needed (a number, a name, a date) — the gap between Band 7 and Band 9 here is mostly preparation, not raw listening speed. By the time the answer is spoken, you should already have it narrowed to two or three options.
  • Spelling has to be exact for Band 9 — a correctly-identified answer with a spelling mistake still loses the mark. Drill the words that get misspelled under pressure (accommodation, environment, necessary, occurred) and double-check plurals.
  • Write down what's actually said, not what you expect to hear — recordings routinely include a distractor answer first, then a correction ("...actually, make that Wednesday").

Reading

  • Budget roughly 20 minutes per passage with a 5-minute buffer at the end for review — and skip a question you're stuck on rather than losing several minutes to one line; you can usually reclaim more marks elsewhere than you'd gain by forcing it.
  • Skim first for the overall structure and main idea of each paragraph, then scan for the specific detail a question is actually asking about — reading every word start to finish is too slow to finish on time.
  • For True/False/Not Given: "Not Given" means the passage simply never addresses the statement either way. The single most common error is marking "False" when the information just isn't there at all.

Writing

  • Address every part of the prompt directly — Task Achievement/Response is graded on whether you actually answered the question asked, not on how sophisticated your vocabulary is elsewhere in the essay.
  • Write past the minimum, not exactly at it: aim for ~170 words on Task 1 and ~280–300 on Task 2, comfortably clear of the 150/250-word thresholds below which you're marked down — a tight word count leaves no margin for a miscount.
  • Don't memorise full essays or paragraphs. Examiners are trained to recognise them, and the usual tell is inconsistent quality — noticeably polished memorised sentences sitting next to weaker original ones — which caps the score well below what your actual level would otherwise earn.
  • Keep cohesion invisible: one or two well-placed connectors per paragraph, not one opening every sentence. See the Coherence and Cohesion note below — it's graded separately from vocabulary and mechanical linking is penalised, not rewarded.
  • Leave the last 2–3 minutes to proofread specifically for the errors you know you tend to make (articles, subject-verb agreement, tense) rather than re-reading passively.

Speaking

  • Extend every answer with a reason or example, then stop once you've actually answered — a Band 9 response reads as a natural conversation, not a monologue or a one-word reply.
  • Don't memorise full answers, especially for Part 2's cue-card topic. It's tempting, but examiners can tell, and a scripted-sounding answer reads as less fluent than an imperfect, natural one.
  • Speak at a natural pace rather than fast — fluency is about ease and smoothness, not speed, and rushing tends to cost coherence.
  • Correcting yourself mid-sentence is normal, natural speech, not a mistake to hide — trying to suppress every self-correction usually costs more fluency than the correction itself.

General preparation

  • Do full, timed mock tests under real conditions, not just isolated question-type drills — time pressure changes how you make decisions, and you want that pressure to feel familiar before test day, not new.
  • Prioritise your weakest section, not your strongest — the Overall Band Score averages all four, so a single weak section drags it down more than a strong one lifts it back up.

Why vocabulary breadth matters for the band descriptors

  • Reading & Listening: many questions (sentence/summary/table completion, matching) hinge on recognising a paraphrase of a word in the passage — the exact word you're tested on rarely appears verbatim in the question. This is exactly why the flashcards emphasize synonyms, not just single-word definitions.
  • Writing — Lexical Resource: one of Writing's four scoring criteria. At Band 8–9 it rewards a wide range of vocabulary used with natural, sophisticated control — uncommon lexical items and precise word choice, with only rare, minor errors. The Idioms & Collocations and Task 1 Data Language topics target this directly.
  • Writing — Coherence and Cohesion: a separate 25%-weighted criterion, easy to conflate with Lexical Resource but graded on its own — how well ideas are logically organised and linked, not how sophisticated the words are. At Band 8–9 the linking "attracts no attention": it should feel invisible, not like a checklist. This is where the Cohesion & Linking topic applies — but overusing connectors (opening every sentence with "Furthermore" or "Moreover") reads as mechanical and is explicitly penalised at the higher bands, so aim for one or two well-placed ones per paragraph rather than working every card you've learned into the essay.
  • Speaking: the Lexical Resource criterion at Band 8–9 similarly requires flexible, precise vocabulary — including idiomatic language — used naturally in conversation, not rehearsed.

Suggested study rhythm

  1. Daily flashcard pass (10–15 min) — pay closest attention to whichever topics show the lowest "mastered" percentage on the Progress tab.
  2. MCQ pass right after, to force active recall in a sentence context — closer to actual Reading/Listening tasks than isolated flashcards.
  3. Once a topic reaches ~80%+ mastered, keep revisiting it in mixed sessions weekly to fight forgetting — mastery fades faster than it feels like it should.
  4. Actively reuse 3–5 new words per week in your own Writing Task 2 practice essays and Speaking part-2 answers — passive recognition (flashcards) and active production (writing/speaking) are different skills, and both need practice.
  5. When you practice a full essay, study the matching Writing-toolkit topic first: Task 1 Data Language before a Task 1 attempt, Idioms & Collocations and Cohesion & Linking before a Task 2 attempt — then check afterward whether you actually used any of them, rather than studying them in isolation and hoping they show up later.
